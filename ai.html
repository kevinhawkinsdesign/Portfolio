On AI

I didn't wait for AI to change design. I went looking for it.

I've spent the last two years building the AI backbone of a global product org, and I've come out the other side more optimistic about craft, not less. This is where I stand, and why.

Why I lean in

AI is a tool, and I've always loved tools.

Twenty years in design has taught me that every generation panics about the same thing: the tool that makes the craft faster will make the craft cheaper, and the craft cheaper will make it disposable. Photoshop didn't kill illustration. 🎨 Figma didn't kill visual design. AI won't kill product design, but it will change, permanently, what's worth spending a senior designer's attention on.

This isn't a one-org thing for me. At Glovo, with Prosus's backing, we folded AI straight into the content and design pipeline: localization, interactive prototyping, even the product imagery and ad assets our partners saw every day. Today I bring the same instinct into the startups I advise, KomboAI and Kabisa among them. But Monta is where I got to run the experiment at full scale. An AI backbone wired into every internal data source. A library of 250+ shared skills. A pipeline that let designers and engineers ship as peers, and a 30-agent system automating 90% of our product lifecycle. Weekly AI usage went from near-zero to 88% of the company in four months. Nobody was mandated to use it. ⚡ They used it because it made them better at the parts of the job they actually cared about.

"The biggest challenge stopped being how do we get people to use AI, and became where should we not use AI."

That's the sentence that stuck with me from that whole project. It's also the reason I don't think the interesting AI conversation is about adoption anymore. It's about judgment: knowing exactly which 10% of the work still needs a human being who has taste, context, and the willingness to be held accountable for a decision.

Prosus, who I worked alongside at Glovo, published research in 2026 showing the same pattern at far bigger scale. Over 60,000 agents built across their portfolio companies, and just 2% of them driving most of the actual business impact. Same judgment problem I keep coming back to. It just gets bigger, not different, the more of an organization you point AI at.

I stay close to a network of people building the frontier of this: folks at Anthropic, OpenAI, and elsewhere thinking hard about what responsible, sustainable AI-assisted creative work actually looks like. Not because I want to be first. Because I want the tools creative people are handed in five years to have been shaped by people who've actually done the craft. 🛠️

✦

How AI is actually changing the PDLC.

The short version: AI is fundamentally transforming the product development lifecycle by compressing prototyping timelines, enabling synthetic user research, and automating code and design generation. That shifts human effort away from manual, pixel-pushing tasks toward deeper strategic problem-solving and systemic orchestration across teams.

01. Prototyping

Weeks become hours

What used to require a sprint of static mocks and a separate build phase to validate now happens in one sitting: high-fidelity, interactive, testable same-day. The bottleneck moves from "can we build it" to "do we know what to build."

02. Research

Synthetic signal, real judgment

AI-assisted synthetic research won't replace talking to real users, but it collapses the time between a hypothesis and a first directional read. Real research time then goes to the questions that actually need a human in the room.

03. Generation

Code and design, together

Design-system-aware generation means the handoff between design and engineering stops being a translation problem. The output isn't a spec anymore: it's a merge-ready PR, and the designer and engineer are reviewing the same thing.

None of this replaces strategic thinking. It makes room for it. The teams that win aren't the ones with the most AI tooling. They're the ones who used the time it freed up to get better at deciding what's worth building at all.

I think about a peer of mine whose design team survived a brutal layoff round by walking into the room with revenue and retention numbers instead of usage or adoption metrics. The same instinct applies here: the right way to justify AI investment isn't "look how much we use it," but the dollar figure it moved. That's the metric I actually track.

What I actually believe
01

Augment the expert, don't replace the craft

The best use of AI I've seen raises the floor for a junior designer and frees a senior one for the judgment calls only they can make. The worst use of AI treats craft as a cost center to be automated away. I build for the first version.

02

Fair and sustainable for creatives

Tools trained on creative work owe something back to the people whose work trained them: in compensation, in attribution, or in access. I care where a tool's training data came from as much as I care what the tool can do.

03

Governance ships with capability

Every rollout I've led shipped monitoring and guardrails alongside the tool, not after it. Speed without oversight isn't velocity: it's just risk you haven't noticed yet.

The tension I sit with

I love this era of AI. I also lose sleep over what it can now see.

I've spent most of my career as a data lover: sponsoring analytics tools as an executive, fighting for instrumentation budget, being the person who actually reads the dashboards. So I understand better than most what AI-native analytics unlocks. Ask a plain-language question, get a real answer, no SQL, no waiting on a data team's backlog. 📊 That shift is genuinely thrilling, and I don't want to undersell how much better it makes decision-making.

But having been the executive sponsor for a dozen of these tools, my worry was never the model itself. It's the access model underneath it. The moment a natural-language layer sits on top of a data warehouse, every permission boundary your data team spent years building either holds or it doesn't. Suddenly anyone in the company can ask a question about a named user they had no business asking before.

A natural-language layer is only ever as safe as the permissions sitting underneath it.

That's why I pay attention to tools like Sundial: they're building the analyst layer with observability and governance as the actual product, not a compliance checkbox bolted on after launch. If AI is going to sit that close to sensitive user data, the tools sitting between the model and the warehouse need to earn that trust deliberately, not accidentally.

I'm not anti-access. I've spent my career trying to get more people closer to data, faster. I just think the teams that win this next wave will be the ones who treat governance as a feature people can feel, not a policy doc nobody reads. 🔐

A framework I keep seeing

The "old way" in that diagram was never the good way.

A framework's been making the rounds that frames AI-native product work as an evolution: an "old way" of linear discovery-to-development hand-offs, and a "new way" of continuous, AI-fed feedback loops. I get why it resonates, and the new-way half is genuinely good practice. But I don't think the interesting shift in that diagram has much to do with AI at all.

Treating product development as a relay race, one team finishes its artifact and throws it over the wall, the next team picks it up cold, was never a pattern worth being nostalgic about. It's the exact failure mode that cross-functional teams, embedded research, and design ops have spent the last decade trying to engineer out of organizations. AI didn't retire that model. Teams still running it just have more urgency to fix it now. 🔁

AI didn't kill the hand-off. Killing the hand-off is what let AI compound.

I didn't need AI to tell me hand-offs were the problem. At Monta, we ran one of the largest Flutter apps outside Google, and the fix wasn't a smarter tool sitting on top of a broken relay: it was retiring the relay itself. Designers and engineers working as peers in the same codebase, no spec thrown over a wall, no waiting on translation. AI is what let that compound faster once the hand-off was already gone. It isn't what got rid of it.

So when I see a diagram like that, my read isn't "AI changed how we work." It's "this team fixed an org design problem, and AI is what made the fix pay off immediately instead of over a few quarters." Worth celebrating. Just don't let the AI take credit for the org chart finally doing its job. 😄

The restructure I keep watching

Generation got cheap. Judgment didn't.

The reasoning behind this year's design layoffs is easy to follow, which is most of the problem. A model drafts the copy. Another fills the frame with mockups. A third turns all of it into something clickable before the kickoff meeting has finished. Watch that once and a smaller design org stops looking like a cut and starts looking like arithmetic. ✂️

The arithmetic holds right until you ask what those people were actually for. I watch the orgs pulling ahead in this window closely, and not one has thinned its design bench. Every one of them has made it more senior instead. Not bigger. Heavier.

That stops being a paradox once you notice what changed. Ten credible directions now land in the time it used to take to sketch one badly, so volume left the list of things worth worrying about. What took its place is the ability to look at those ten and know which one survives contact with a real user, a real roadmap, and a real engineering budget. Then defend the call well enough that a room agrees to fund it. It's the same sliver of the work I keep pointing at everywhere else on this page: the part no amount of tooling absorbs, and the part everyone is currently cutting.

That capacity is what seniority actually buys, and no prompt produces it, because it accrues rather than downloads. Ship enough products and you learn which compromises come back for you. Own enough decisions and you can name a tradeoff before it turns into a support queue. Sit through enough reviews where being wrong cost you something, and recognizing the pattern early stops feeling like instinct and starts behaving like a skill. None of that fits in a context window.

I've run the opposite version of this decision, and it worked. At Monta I inherited a design organization I was never going to be allowed to grow. Instead of doing less with fewer, I rewrote every role definition, level framework, and job description around what an AI-native team actually needs from people. The seat count never moved. The bar did. 📐

The scarce thing was never the making. It was knowing what deserved to exist.

Which is why this particular restructure optimizes the wrong layer. You retain everyone fluent in operating the machine and release the handful of people qualified to tell you the machine is wrong. The tooling budget goes up. The quality ceiling comes down, quietly, over about two quarters.

The lag is what makes the judgment gap so expensive, because it never announces itself. Throughput climbs, dashboards look healthy, more ships than ever shipped before. The bill arrives later as a feature nobody asked for, a flow that tests clean and converts badly, a roadmap with no one senior enough left on it to say no.

Inside a single month, 15 of these conversations reached me, from CDOs, CHROs, and CPOs: chiefs of design, human resources, and product. Nearly all of them were opening a VP of Design search not long after closing one. Different companies, different sectors, the same sentence delivered with the same rueful timing: "We thought we needed less senior leadership, not more."

If you signed off on a decision like this and haven't yet said out loud that it might have been wrong, I understand the hesitation. Unwinding a restructure reads publicly as an admission, and nobody schedules that meeting willingly. The consolation is that this correction is close to invisible when it happens early. Catch the gap while it's still a staffing question and it stays one. Leave it, and it becomes a product decision, and those are the ones you end up explaining to the board. ⚖️

9. Turn a prompt that works into a team skill

A skill is a folder with a SKILL.md file: a name, a description that says when to use it and when not to, ordered instructions, and edge cases. This one packages the token audit from prompt 12 below. In Claude Code, save it as .claude/skills/ds-audit/SKILL.md. In Cursor, run /create-skill and paste it in. Do not start from zero: Figma ships official skills with its plugin, and the collections above give you more. Build one team skill a month and you have a real design ops toolkit by summer.

---
name: ds-audit
description: Audit Figma frames against the design system. Use for token, variable or component drift. Not for new designs.
---

# Design system audit

## When to use
When someone asks to check frames for hardcoded values or drift from the library.

## Instructions
1. Use the figma-use skill before any use_figma call.
2. List hardcoded colors, type, radius and spacing in the selection.
3. Match each to the closest library variable, with a confidence level.
4. Output a table. Change nothing until the user approves.
5. Fix high-confidence items only; report the rest.

## Examples
"Audit the booking widget frames" returns a table of layer, current value, suggested variable, confidence.

## Common edge cases
- No close match: flag it as "needs new token", never invent one.
- Component is detached: report it, don't reattach without approval.

10. The prompt library

Copy, fill the brackets, run. Every prompt is ready to paste into Figma, Claude, Gemini or Cursor. Replace anything in [square brackets] with your own context first.

Three habits make every prompt better: give the model your real context (a Project with research, your Figma library, your repo), ask it to mark what it inferred or guessed, and check numbers and quotes yourself before anything ships. Anonymize participant data according to your company's policy before uploading it. Generation got cheap. Judgment didn't.

01 Discover AI-accelerated research

1A competitive scan that shows its sources

Try it in Gemini Deep Research, or Claude with Research on.

You are a UX researcher for a [your product category] platform. Research how [competitor 1], [competitor 2], [competitor 3], [competitor 4] and [competitor 5] design [the feature you are studying] for [your user type] with [typical scale]. For each product cover: [aspect 1], [aspect 2], and [aspect 3]. Return a comparison table with a source link for every claim. If you can't find evidence, write "no evidence found". Finish with 5 patterns worth testing and 3 to avoid.
2Interview synthesis you can trust

Try it in a Claude Project or a Gemini Gem with the transcripts attached.

Attached are [8] interview transcripts with [your user type]. Synthesize them:
1. Up to 8 themes. For each: how many participants mentioned it, 2 verbatim quotes with participant IDs, and any evidence against it.
2. Separate what people say they do from what they describe actually doing.
3. Mark anything you inferred rather than read.
Never invent or paraphrase inside quotation marks. End with 3 questions for our next round.
3Simulate a user, then list what to validate

Try it in Claude or Gemini. A simulated user is a sketch of a person, never a substitute for one.

Role-play a property manager in Lisbon with 40 short-term listings, 2 cleaners and 1 assistant. It's Sunday night before a big conference week. Walk me through, hour by hour, how you decide which rates to change and which tools you open. Then step out of the role and list the 10 assumptions in your story that I should validate with real users, ranked by risk.

02 Define AI-enhanced framing

4From themes to job stories and HMWs

Try it in Claude or Gemini, in the same Project or Gem as prompt 2 so it sees the evidence.

Here are our research themes: [paste]. Write:
1. 3 job stories (When..., I want to..., so I can...)
2. 3 problem statements naming the user, the need and the evidence
3. 5 How-might-we questions, from safe to bold
Rate each problem statement's confidence (low, medium, high) and tell me what evidence would change your mind. Push back if a theme is too thin to frame.
5Counterfactuals: what if the constraint disappeared?

Try it in Gemini or Claude. Swap in your own constraints: budget, channel rules, owner approval, regulation.

Our problem statement: [paste]. Run 4 counterfactuals:
1. What if changing prices were free and instant?
2. What if the manager had no historical data?
3. What if the AI could act without approval?
4. What if we had to ship in one week?
For each: what would we design differently, and what does that reveal about the real constraint?
6Define the role of AI in the product

Try it in Claude or Gemini, before designing any AI agent screen.

We're designing an AI pricing agent for property managers. For each action it could take (raise rates, lower rates, block dates, reply to a guest), recommend if it should be assistive (suggests), augmentative (acts after approval) or autonomous (acts, then reports). Give the risk if it's wrong, how we explain the decision, and how the user undoes it. Output a table.

03 Develop AI co-creation

7Three directions on your design system

Try it in the Figma agent with your libraries enabled. Run A, B and C as parallel prompts so they don't converge.

Using our [your] library, create 3 directions for a Pricing suggestions experience on the multi-calendar:
A) inline in the calendar cells
B) a side panel
C) a morning digest
Use only library components and variables. For each direction show default, empty and error states, and explain how the user sees why a rate was suggested. Put each direction in its own labeled frame.
8Weeks become hours: a prototype from one brief

Try it in Claude, Claude Design, a Figma code layer or Figma Make. Check every number it shows: in my test run, one projected total was off by 10 euros.

Build an interactive, single-screen prototype: "Pricing suggestions" for a property manager with 40 listings in Lisbon and Barcelona. An AI pricing agent proposes rate changes for the next 14 days. Show each suggestion with current rate, suggested rate, % change, the reason in plain language and confidence in words. Let the user accept, edit or dismiss each one, and bulk-accept high-confidence ones, with 10 seconds to undo. Include loading, empty and error states. Explain the agent's guardrails. Use an 8px grid, WCAG AA contrast and realistic data. No lorem ipsum.
9Map the journey before the screens

Try it in Claude or Gemini, or the Figma agent in FigJam once it arrives.

Before any screens, map the end-to-end journey for a property manager onboarding a new listing in [your product], from signing the owner to the first booking. For each step: the screen or channel, what the user needs to know, what can go wrong, and where AI could help or harm. Then propose the 6 screens to design first, and why.
10Campaign concepts with one visual language (marketing)

Try it in Gemini for copy and images, then finish in Figma with Weave tools. Treat images as concepts; brand review still applies.

Create 4 ad concepts for [your product] aimed at property managers with 10 to 50 listings in Southern Europe. Core message: pricing that keeps working while you sleep. For each concept: a headline (max 6 words), a subline (max 12 words), the objection it answers, and the visual idea.
Then write one image prompt per concept in a consistent style: warm natural light, real apartment interiors, no faces looking at the camera, 4:5 crop, empty space at the top for the headline.

04 Deliver AI-integrated execution

11Implement a frame with your real components

Try it in Cursor or Claude Code with the Figma MCP server connected. In Figma, right-click a layer and choose Copy link to selection.

Implement this design: [Figma link to selection].
Use our existing components, following the Code Connect mappings, and our design tokens. Never hardcode colors, spacing or radii. Build every state shown in the frame.
When you finish, list any element you couldn't map to a component and any value you had to guess.
12Audit tokens without the manual hunt

Try it in the Figma agent, or Claude Code with the Figma MCP server.

Audit the selected frames against our library. List every layer that uses a hardcoded color, font size, radius or spacing value instead of a variable. For each one, suggest the closest variable and your confidence.
Don't change anything yet: output a table. After I approve, fix only the high-confidence ones and report the rest.
13Accessibility QA before engineering starts

Try it in Cursor or Claude Code. Automated checks catch only part of accessibility issues; test with assistive tech and real users too.

Review this component for WCAG 2.2 AA. Compute the contrast of every text and background pair from the actual token values, not from the screenshot. Check focus order, keyboard access, screen reader labels, target size (at least 24 by 24 px) and reduced motion.
List issues by severity with the fix. Then write a Playwright and axe test that would catch them.
14A user test plan, and the readout after

Try it in Claude or Gemini, then run the test in Maze.

Write a 20-minute unmoderated test plan for the pricing suggestions prototype, for property managers who run 10+ listings. Include a screener, 5 tasks with success criteria, and 2 follow-up questions per task. Avoid leading language.
After the test I'll paste results: summarize by task with completion, time on task, verbatim quotes and the top 3 fixes.

11. Five rituals that protect ownership and collaboration

RitualWhat it looks like
1. Show the prompt in critsShare the agent thread with the design, so others see how you got there.
2. Diverge alone, converge togetherGenerate options with AI, choose the direction as a team.
